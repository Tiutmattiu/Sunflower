# Sunflower — World Lore Freestyle Notes

> Companion to `GAME_DESIGN.md`, `SCENARIOS_TEACHING.md`, `NPC_ENGINE_ROUTE_AUDIT.md`, and `NPC_CHARACTER_BIBLE.md`.
>
> This document captures the September 2026 worldbuilding freestyle. These are **promising narrative hypotheses, not locked canon**. The purpose is to preserve the ideas while preventing lore from swallowing the trading game.

---

## 1. Core Worldbuilding Direction

The harbour economy may exist inside an intentionally absurd, semi-closed civilisation where fresh food, movement, bodily transformation, social isolation, and commodity dependence are linked.

Possible core premise:

- people in the settlement rarely leave protected indoor / urban spaces;
- going outside exposes them to a mysterious process that can transform humans into animals;
- many inhabitants therefore survive through preserved / canned food and delivered goods;
- fresh food is unusually scarce and strategically valuable;
- already-transformed animal people may be pushed toward informal / black-market networks;
- legitimate and illegitimate markets may therefore overlap with social exclusion rather than simple morality;
- the player may initially misunderstand the world and their own role in it.

This should remain **subtext and discovery**, not an exposition dump before trading begins.

---

## 2. Sardines as Currency — Narrative Origin

The sardine currency began as a personal joke / emotional origin: the original creator chose sardines because her boyfriend routinely ate canned sardines on specific weekdays.

For the finished game, this can be re-grounded diegetically.

Promising direction:

### Canned / preserved food as commodity money

Fresh food is hard to obtain, transport, and store safely. Preserved fish is:

- nutritionally useful;
- durable;
- divisible into standard containers;
- widely demanded;
- transportable;
- storable;
- accepted across social classes because everyone ultimately needs food.

Therefore `🥫 sardines` can function as a **commodity money / settlement good** rather than arbitrary coins.

Vale accepting sardine tins is then not a joke she tolerates; it reflects a world where preserved nutrition is one of the most reliable stores of practical value.

This does **not** require every sardine tin to be produced freely by Fishmonger. Production must have costs and supply limits.

---

## 3. Fresh Food as Scarcity

Possible world rule:

- fresh food is rare;
- preserved food dominates daily life;
- people may rely heavily on tins, capsules, concentrates, preserved ingredients, and delivered provisions;
- traders with access to fresh fish, citrus, herbs, fruit, or other fresh inputs can create genuine scarcity rents.

This makes existing items more meaningful:

- Lime Crate is not a generic quest key;
- Fresh Mackerel is not merely a `perishable=true` object;
- Bruised Mint can still matter because even damaged fresh plant matter may be scarce;
- Grandma's meal becomes narratively important because cooking real food can itself be socially unusual.

Freshness should therefore carry both **economic** and **cultural** value.

---

## 4. Human-to-Animal Transformation Hypothesis

A major speculative idea:

- humans who go outside / cross certain boundaries risk transformation into animals;
- Dock Dog may once have been human;
- other animal NPCs may also have former human identities;
- the mechanism may involve infection, environment, deliberate social control, or something more surreal;
- the black market may be disproportionately populated by already-transformed beings who no longer benefit from staying inside.

Do **not** lock the cause yet.

Important thematic possibilities:

- transformation as literal biological risk;
- transformation as social exclusion;
- transformation as adaptation;
- transformation as a price of freedom / movement;
- transformation as a consequence of the settlement's economic system;
- transformation as something the official story misrepresents.

Avoid turning animal characters into a simple oppressed-class allegory unless the final narrative supports it with nuance.

## 5. Dock Dog — Revised Direction

Dock Dog should **not** necessarily be defined as the poorest character.

Better economic identity:

- high inflow;
- high outflow;
- extremely high turnover;
- recurring consumption because of the cats;
- frequently converts goods into other goods rather than accumulating wealth;
- may appear perpetually cash-light while moving significant volume.

Dog's role represents a type of market participant whose business is based on:

- turnover;
- scavenging;
- low margins;
- informal access;
- information;
- recycling / repurposing;
- recurring consumption needs.

Dog does **not** need a theft mechanic by default. Theft should only exist if it creates meaningful economic concepts (property rights, provenance, stolen-goods discount, fencing, insurance, moral hazard, etc.). Otherwise it adds complexity without teaching much.

Dog may have once been human and already cared for cats before transformation.

## 6. NPCs as Market-Participant Archetypes

A new design goal has emerged:

> Each major NPC should represent a recognisable **type of market participant / trading style**, not merely a profession.

Their narrative biography should make that style rational.

Possible spectrum to cover across the cast:

- high-turnover dealer;
- novice / unsophisticated participant;
- safe / institutional actor;
- leveraged speculator;
- sophisticated opportunist;
- relationship trader;
- asset-rich capital allocator;
- distressed / liquidity-constrained actor;
- information broker;
- long-term operator / producer.

The character should **embody** the archetype through life circumstances rather than explicitly announcing it.

Do not force every common market type into exactly one NPC if that makes characters artificial. Some dimensions can overlap.

## 7. Fishmonger — Two Competing Directions

Fishmonger is currently unresolved and should **not** be finalised yet.

### Direction A — Institutional / monetary anchor

Fishmonger could represent safety, authority, stability, or a quasi-public monetary institution.

Possible functions:

- controlled issue / distribution of preserved fish currency;
- stabilising local supply;
- responding to shortages;
- influencing currency abundance;
- acting as an exchange point;
- embodying the benefits and constraints of centralised market governance.

This direction would make Fishmonger closer to a monetary authority / regulated institution than a normal merchant.

### Direction B — Fish trader with a hidden escape motive

Fishmonger could instead remain a private fish trader who wants to accumulate enough capital to buy a ship and leave.

Potential mystery:

- why can Fishmonger work outside so regularly without suffering animal transformation?
- legitimate traders, black-market actors, and the player may all want to learn this secret;
- Fishmonger may actively protect the secret and seek escape before it becomes public.

### Direction C — Fishmonger is not human

A more surreal possibility: Fishmonger is themselves an octopus / marine being selling sea life and recovered objects from an aquatic economy.

This could make their apparent immunity to the outside world trivial rather than conspiratorial.

**Do not combine A, B, and C automatically.** Choose later based on the final conspiracy/world logic.

## 8. Multi-Currency / FX — Keep as Expansion, Not Core Yet

The world could plausibly support multiple preserved-food currencies or foreign settlement goods.

Potential future mechanics:

- local sardine tins as primary unit of account;
- foreign currencies / commodity monies brought by sailors;
- fluctuating exchange rates due to seasonal catch, scarcity, and trade flows;
- legitimate exchange vs black-market exchange;
- FX arbitrage;
- policy / supply intervention by a monetary authority if Fishmonger takes the institutional role.

However, do not add multiple currencies to early 1.0 unless the core market remains legible.

## 9. Sailor — Unstable Narrative Role, Useful Economic Function

The Sailor's narrative identity is now deliberately open.

Possible readings:

### Ordinary human outsider
The Sailor may be one of the few recognisably normal humans and view the settlement as strange / dangerous.

### Explorer / external interface
The Sailor may connect the enclosed harbour economy to the outside world.

### Functional voyage agent
If the player later acquires businesses or productive assets, the Sailor can be commissioned to source external inputs, transport goods, or undertake expeditions.

This makes the Sailor a natural system for:

- external supply;
- voyage risk;
- import lead times;
- pre-orders / forward contracts;
- commissioning;
- shipping / logistics;
- inventory planning;
- trade finance.

The Sailor should leave and potentially return later in the run. Return timing may be partly predictable / contractible rather than purely random.

Potential late-run design:

> The Sailor leaves mid-game and returns near the end with commissioned or opportunistically acquired goods.

This creates anticipation and cross-round planning.

## 10. Vale — Ruthless but Reliable

Vale's interpersonal unpleasantness should **not** imply poor contractual reliability.

Strong direction:

- manipulative;
- willing to exploit asymmetric information;
- willing to lie strategically;
- willing to use insider networks;
- highly goal-directed;
- socially exploitative;
- not sentimental;
- but usually honours formal settlement and contracts.

This allows a useful lesson:

> A person can be unpleasant, self-interested, and manipulative while still being a highly reliable counterparty.

Conversely, a warm person can be a terrible credit risk.

Sunflower should avoid a good-person = good-counterparty equation.

## 11. Clown — Possible Truth-Knower

Clown is becoming a potential major narrative pivot.

Current hypotheses:

- others think Clown is insane;
- Clown may actually understand something true about the world;
- jumping / racing toward the cliff may connect to leaving the settlement;
- the apparent “crazy” behaviour may be based on information others do not have;
- Clown's financial risk-taking and narrative risk-taking can mirror each other.

Possible revelation:

> The cliff is not merely a race location. Clown believes — or knows — that crossing / jumping it is a route out of the enclosed world.

This would make the Cliff route much more than a gambling minigame.

Do not yet decide whether Clown is literally the only normal person.

## 12. Clown's Debt — Still Open

Clown's debt should matter, but the creditor is not yet fixed.

Candidate structures:

- debt to a seagull / black-market lender;
- multiple small debts;
- debt secured against future racing winnings;
- debt related to attempted purchase of the bar;
- obligation to Vale;
- debt to a previous bar owner;
- no literal debt at the start, but aggressive leverage as the game progresses.

Choose the structure that creates the richest interaction with:

- credit access;
- black-market finance;
- asset acquisition;
- race incentives;
- relationship network.

## 13. Bar Apprentice — Rich Naive Partner

The Apprentice can become much more than a cocktail-production NPC.

Promising identity:

- young / university-age;
- sheltered;
- from a wealthy family;
- possibly related to the current bar ownership structure;
- genuinely kind;
- inexperienced at trading / investing;
- loves bartending, social life, and exploration;
- wants to see the outside world;
- may want a ship or partnership rather than merely money.

The Apprentice could be one of the **worst traders initially**.

This enables:

- the player teaching an NPC;
- mentorship;
- partnership;
- co-investment;
- agency delegation;
- company formation;
- the Apprentice becoming stronger because of the player's teaching;
- the possibility that the Apprentice eventually outgrows / competes with the player.

Potential relationship:

> player + Apprentice form a partnership / trading company / voyage venture.

This should remain optional, not mandatory.

## 14. Bar Ownership as Investment Case

The bar can become a real asset rather than background scenery.

Current possibilities:

- owner absent and looking to sell;
- Apprentice is a relative / helper rather than owner;
- Grandma may hold some equity or family claim;
- Clown wants to acquire it;
- player may potentially invest;
- Vale may view it purely as an asset;
- Apprentice may care more about its social / cultural future than its price.

This allows a genuine investment case:

- revenue;
- costs;
- lease / property risk;
- equipment;
- customer base;
- sentimental value;
- hidden liabilities;
- ownership structure;
- competing bidders;
- financing.

It also creates a natural bridge between trading and longer-term ownership.

## 15. Grandma / Bar / Clown / Player Conspiracy Cluster

A major story possibility is emerging around:

- Grandma;
- the bar;
- Clown;
- the player;
- the sunflower;
- the truth of the settlement.

This cluster may hold the deeper narrative revelation while Vale / Dog / Fishmonger / Sailor expose other pieces of the world.

No final conspiracy is locked.

Potential questions:

- why does Grandma have sunflowers?
- why is the bar connected to leaving / remembering / fresh food?
- what does Clown know?
- why is the player here?
- what happened to the original Sunflower Town?
- is the sunflower a cure, key, infection, commodity, symbol, or all of these in different interpretations?

## 16. Sunflower as Ambiguous Object

The sunflower should **not** necessarily be unambiguously good.

Current speculative possibilities:

### Restoration
Planting sunflower seeds may restore biological / social life to the settlement.

### Contamination
The seeds may carry the same process that corrupted / transformed the current world. Bringing them elsewhere could spread the system.

### Commodity extraction
The player's employer / institution may want the sunflower as a collectible, specimen, patentable resource, museum object, or high-value acquisition.

### Choice after acquisition
Obtaining the sunflower does not have to end the moral / narrative decision.

The player may choose to:

- plant it locally;
- take it away;
- sell it;
- donate it;
- lock it in a collection / museum;
- destroy it;
- distribute the seeds;
- commercialise it.

These should not be reduced to good / evil endings. They can create different consequences and reveal the player's relationship to value, ownership, growth, and control.

## 17. Capitalism / Contagion Theme — Use Carefully

A possible thematic direction is that Sunflower Town once had more direct contact with life, food, community, and the outside world, but gradually became dependent on preserved commodities, enclosed living, financial exchange, and mediated relationships.

The sunflower may symbolise:

- lost biological abundance;
- reproduction / spreading;
- capital accumulation;
- commodity circulation;
- cultural memory;
- a pathogen / idea that reproduces itself;
- the possibility of regeneration or renewed colonisation.

The player may discover they are not an innocent rescuer but an agent of a larger institution seeking to extract, collect, commodify, or propagate the sunflower.

**Do not write the theme as “capitalism bad.”**

The trading system itself should remain intellectually honest: markets can coordinate, exploit, provide freedom, create dependence, reward skill, transmit information, and fail under different conditions.

The narrative should create tension between:

- exchange and gift;
- ownership and stewardship;
- efficiency and dependence;
- mobility and safety;
- accumulation and circulation;
- preservation and living growth.

## 18. World Layer vs Market Layer

Critical scope rule:

### Market layer must work without knowing the conspiracy.

A player should be able to enjoy:

- valuation;
- negotiation;
- information;
- credit;
- arbitrage;
- black markets;
- auctions;
- asset acquisition;
- NPC learning;

without understanding the deeper lore.

### World layer should reinterpret the same mechanics.

Later revelations may make the player rethink:

- why sardines are money;
- why fresh food matters;
- why animals dominate the black market;
- why Clown races toward the cliff;
- why Grandma has sunflower seeds;
- who benefits from keeping people indoors;
- what the player's institution actually wants.

The lore should **recontextualise** gameplay, not replace it.

---

## 19. Current Strongest Narrative Thesis

A promising high-level structure is:

> At first, the player believes they have entered a strange market to acquire a rare sunflower.
>
> As they learn to trade, they also learn why this market exists.
>
> By the time they can obtain the sunflower, they understand that acquisition is not a neutral act.

This preserves the trading game as the primary activity while allowing the final choice to carry thematic weight.

## 20. Important Unresolved Decisions

Do not implement these as canon yet:

1. What exactly causes animal transformation?
2. Are transformed animals harmed, liberated, or simply changed?
3. Is Fishmonger a public institution, private trader, non-human outsider, or something else?
4. Is the Sailor an ordinary human, external-world agent, alien-like outsider, or functional logistics role?
5. Is Clown actually correct about the cliff?
6. Who does Clown owe and why?
7. What is the player's employer / origin?
8. Why does the player specifically need the sunflower?
9. What is Grandma's relationship to the old town / sunflower?
10. Is the sunflower cure, contagion, key, commodity, or ambiguous biological object?
11. What happened historically to make canned food and enclosure normal?
12. Is the Authority protecting people from a real danger, exploiting fear, or both?
13. Are black-market animal communities more free, more vulnerable, or both?
14. What does “leaving” the world actually mean?
15. What happens after the player acquires the sunflower?

These are now the highest-value narrative questions.
